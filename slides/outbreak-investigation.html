Forty staff are sick by Tuesday evening. What do you do first?

Outbreak investigation, followed one step at a time through a single hospital outbreak

Department of Community and Family Medicine, AIIMS Bhopal · 60 minutes

Good morning. For the next hour we will follow one outbreak from the first phone call to the final report. It happens in a hospital like ours, and it lands on the desk of someone like you: the hospital manager. I will stop often and ask you what you would do. There are no wrong answers in those pauses. The case is invented for teaching, but every step in it is how real outbreaks are investigated.

An outbreak is a management problem before it is a lab result

When staff or patients fall sick together, the manager feels it first.

Beds

Wards close or stop admitting. Surgery lists get cancelled.

Staff

Rosters collapse overnight. Sick staff must stay away from patients.

Patients

Vulnerable patients can be infected by staff, food or equipment.

Trust

Media, regulators and families ask one question: who knew, and when?

The epidemiologist finds the cause. The manager makes the response possible.

Why should a hospital manager learn outbreak investigation? Because an outbreak is first felt as a management problem. Beds close. Rosters fall apart. Patients are put at risk. And then the phone rings from a reporter or the district office. You will not run the laboratory tests yourself. But you will decide how fast the team moves, who is excluded from work, what gets reported and to whom. So you need to understand the logic well enough to support it and to question it.

When does "a few sick people" become an outbreak?

Ask the room first. Then compare with this.

Outbreak

More cases than expected, in a given place, population and time.

Baseline

The usual number of cases. You cannot say "more" without knowing "usual".

Cluster

Cases grouped in place or time. It may or may not be above baseline.

Two cases of a disease you never see can be an outbreak. Twenty cases of the common cold may not be.

Let us start with a question. When does a few sick people become an outbreak? Take a moment. You will hear answers like "when many people are sick". The key word in the definition is expected. An outbreak is more cases than expected, for that place, that population and that time. That means you need a baseline, the usual number. A single case of cholera in a hospital kitchen may be an outbreak. Twenty colds in winter probably are not. A cluster is simply cases grouped together. It becomes an outbreak once you show it is above baseline.

Here is our hospital. A 900 bed teaching hospital. It is Tuesday morning, 9 am. The duty manager tells you twenty-nine staff have called in sick with diarrhoea, and seven were seen in Emergency overnight. Normally you get two or three sick calls a week for tummy trouble. Before anything else: is this outbreak real?

Twenty-nine against a usual two or three looks real. What else could make the numbers jump?

Check the diagnosis

Talk to the sick. See the clinical notes. Send stool samples today. Is it one illness or several?

Rule out a pseudo-outbreak

A new lab test, a new reporting rule, a festival leave pattern, or lab contamination can all create cases on paper.

Here: same symptoms, same night, far above baseline. Treat it as real and keep checking.

Before you mobilise everyone, ask: is this real? Put the question to the room. What else could make numbers jump? Good answers: a new lab test that detects more, a change in how sick leave is reported, people calling in sick around a holiday, or contamination in the lab that makes negative samples look positive. That is called a pseudo-outbreak, an apparent increase that is not a true increase in illness. So two checks: confirm the diagnosis by talking to patients and sending samples, and rule out artefacts. In our case, same symptoms, same night, ten times the baseline. We treat it as real.

Textbooks list the steps of an outbreak investigation. This version has twelve and comes from the CDC. Do not read it as a queue where step ten waits for step nine. In real life several run at once. Control, in green, starts on day one, as soon as you have a reasonable suspicion. Communication also starts early. We will walk through these steps with our canteen outbreak. By the end you will have seen every one of them.

Who counts as a case? Write it before you count

A case definition has four parts. Try drafting ours.

PartOur working definition
PersonAny member of hospital staff

TimeOnset between Monday 12:00 and Wednesday 12:00
ClinicalThree or more loose stools in 24 hours

Add a lab tier later: confirmed (stool grows the organism), probable (meets the clinical definition, no lab result).

Do not put the suspected food in the definition. You cannot then test whether the food caused it.

Next, who counts as a case? A case definition is a standard set of criteria for deciding whether a person has the illness under investigation. It always has person, place, time and clinical features. Ask the room to draft one before you show this. Notice what is not in it: the canteen. If you define a case as "someone who ate at the canteen and got diarrhoea", you have assumed the answer and can no longer test it. Early on, keep the definition broad so you miss no one. Later you can add confirmed and probable tiers once lab results come in.

Once you have a definition, you search for cases: Emergency records, sick leave, staff health clinic, and asking around. Each case goes into a line list, a table with one row per person and one column per fact. Name, age, job, department, when symptoms started, what symptoms, lab result. By Wednesday our team has 48 cases. The line list is the single most useful thing a manager can insist on. Every graph and every table that follows comes from it. If the data are messy here, everything after is messy.

What does this shape tell you?

Illustrative figures, constructed for teaching. 48 cases.

This is an epidemic curve, or epi curve. It is a histogram of cases by time of onset. Each box is one person. Look at it for a moment and tell me what you see. Where does it start? Where is the peak? How quickly does it end? Is there one peak or several? Let the room talk. Some will say it rose fast and fell fast. Some will say everyone got sick around the same time. Hold those answers. The next slide explains what they mean.

One sharp peak points to one shared exposure

Salmonella usually takes 12 to 36 hours to cause symptoms (range 6 to 72). Count back from the median onset: the exposure falls between Sunday evening and Monday evening. Monday lunch sits inside.

Incubation period: Heymann DL, ed. Control of Communicable Diseases Manual, 21st ed. APHA Press, 2022.

A rapid rise, a single peak and a quick fall means everyone was exposed at about the same time to the same source. This is called a point source outbreak. A continuing source, like a contaminated water tank, gives a plateau. Person to person spread gives successive waves. Now the useful trick. The incubation period is the time from exposure to first symptoms. By Tuesday afternoon the lab reports Salmonella in the first stool samples. Salmonella usually takes 12 to 36 hours. Our median onset is Tuesday 07:30. Count back 12 to 36 hours and you land between Sunday evening and Monday evening. What did many staff share in that window? Monday lunch at the staff canteen.

Time told us when. Now place and person. If the cases were all in one ward, we would look at that ward: its water, its equipment, its patients. Here they are spread across nine departments, and across every kind of job. That tells us the ward is not the source. Something these people share is. And look at who is not sick: night staff and people who bring food from home. When you describe time, place and person, the gaps are often as informative as the cases.

Our suspect is Monday lunch. How do we test it?

A hypothesis is a testable guess about source and route. Ours: something served at Monday lunch in the staff canteen.

Cohort study, used here

The canteen's meal tokens list 240 people who ate. Interview them all about what they ate, ill or well. We reached 180.

Case-control study

When you cannot list everyone exposed, compare cases with similar people who stayed well.

Both designs do the same thing: compare the ill with the well.

Illustrative figures, constructed for teaching.

A hypothesis is a testable guess about the source and how it spread. Ours is Monday lunch. Now we need to test it with data, not opinion. The canteen issues meal tokens, so we have a list of everyone who ate: 240 people. That is a defined group, a cohort. We interview all of them, sick or not, about each dish they ate. We reached 180. When you cannot list everyone exposed, like a wedding with no guest list, you do a case-control study instead: interview cases and a group of similar people who stayed well. Both designs compare ill with well.

Attack rate: the share of exposed people who fell ill

Attack rate= people who ate the dish and fell illall people who ate the dish × 100

It is a proportion over the whole outbreak, not a rate per unit of time. The name is historical.

Chicken biryani= 4296 =43.8%

Compute it twice for every dish: once for those who ate it, once for those who did not.

Illustrative figures, constructed for teaching.

The main measure here is the attack rate. It is the number who ate a dish and fell ill, divided by everyone who ate that dish, times one hundred. Despite the name, it is a proportion, not a rate, because there is no time unit in it. For the chicken biryani, 42 of the 96 people who ate it fell ill: 43.8 percent. But a single number means nothing on its own. We need the same calculation for people who did not eat the biryani. That comparison is the whole test.

The relative risk divides the attack rate in eaters by the attack rate in non-eaters. One means the dish made no difference. Above one means eaters were more likely to fall ill. For biryani it is 6.1: six times the risk. The confidence interval, 2.7 to 13.7, tells us chance alone is an unlikely explanation. And 42 of our 48 cases ate it, so it explains most of the outbreak. Veg pulao looks protective, with a relative risk below one. That is because people who chose pulao mostly did not take biryani. Raita is still at 2.4. Is raita a second culprit? Next slide.

Illustrative figures, constructed for teaching.

Raita came with the biryani. Most people who took one took the other. So is raita dangerous, or is it just keeping bad company? We split the table. Among people who ate biryani, those who took raita and those who did not got sick at the same rate. Among those who skipped biryani, raita did not raise risk either. So raita is cleared. This is called confounding: a third factor, here the biryani, is linked to both raita and illness and creates a false association. In real outbreaks this is common, because dishes are served together. Always ask what else travels with your suspect.

A strong association is not yet proof

The numbers point at the biryani. Three other lines of evidence must agree.

Laboratory

Salmonella in 19 of 23 stool samples. Same organism in the leftover biryani sample.

Kitchen

Chicken cooked on Sunday night, left at room temperature for five hours, reheated on Monday.

Food handlers

Stool samples from all kitchen staff. One cook had mild diarrhoea last week.

Epidemiology, lab and kitchen findings tell the same story. Now the conclusion is safe.

Illustrative figures, constructed for teaching.

A relative risk of six is strong evidence, but it is still statistics. We want the laboratory, the kitchen and the people to tell the same story. Stool samples: 19 of 23 grew Salmonella. The leftover biryani, which the canteen was told to keep on Tuesday morning, grew the same organism. The kitchen inspection found the chicken was cooked on Sunday night and left out for five hours before reheating. That is the classic setting for bacteria to multiply. And kitchen staff are screened. When three independent lines agree, you can be confident.

Waiting for proof while the source is still active is the most common and costly mistake.

Exclusion period as per the hospital infection control policy. Timeline illustrative.

Here is the most important point for managers. Control does not wait for the relative risk. On Tuesday morning, with only a strong suspicion, we stopped leftovers and kept food samples. Sick staff were kept away from patients and food handling until 48 hours after their symptoms stopped. That matters most in places like the NICU. The roster was rebuilt and non-urgent surgery postponed where needed. On Wednesday, once the kitchen findings came in, deeper measures followed. Early action based on a reasonable suspicion can be adjusted later. Late action cannot undo cases.

Who needs to hear, and in what order?

Inside, first hours

Medical superintendent, Hospital Infection Control Committee, heads of affected departments.

Public health, same day

District Surveillance Unit under IDSP, the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme.

Staff and patients

What happened, what to do if unwell, who to call. Plain and short.

Media

One spokesperson. Facts, actions taken, next update time.

Silence gets filled by rumour. Tell people early, tell them what you know and what you do not know yet.

Communication is part of the response, not an afterthought. Inside the hospital, the medical superintendent and the Hospital Infection Control Committee hear within hours. Public health authorities hear the same day. In India that means the District Surveillance Unit of the Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme, IDSP. They may send a rapid response team and they need to know if cases appear outside the hospital. Staff need plain instructions. And the media get one spokesperson with facts and a time for the next update. Say what you know, and say what you do not know yet.

Our example was food. Most of the outbreaks you will manage are inside the wards: a cluster of bloodstream infections in the NICU, or a resistant organism on an ICU. A healthcare-associated infection is one that was not present or incubating when the patient was admitted. The logic is the same: confirm against baseline, define cases, line list, describe, hypothesise, test, control, communicate. What changes is the detail. The baseline comes from your routine infection surveillance. The curve often builds slowly. The sources are hands, fluids and equipment. With few cases, a case-control study is common. And control means cohorting and audits. That is why surveillance data matter before any outbreak.

What must exist before the next outbreak?

Before

A named outbreak team with authority. Baseline surveillance data. A written outbreak policy. Lab and IDSP contacts.

During

Daily team meeting. One line list. Decision log with times. Staff roster backup. One spokesperson.

After

Written report. Fixes assigned with owners and dates. Follow-up audit.

The quality of your response on Tuesday was decided months earlier.

What should a manager put in place now, before anything happens? An outbreak team with authority to act, baseline surveillance data so you can tell when numbers rise, a written policy, and contacts for the lab and the district. During an outbreak, a daily meeting, one shared line list, and a log of decisions with times. That log protects you later. After it ends, a report and a list of fixes with owners and dates. Our Tuesday went well only because some of this was ready.

An outbreak ends with a report, not with relief

The outbreak is over when cases return to baseline for twice the longest incubation period. For Salmonella, about six days.

The report says

What happened, how we know, what we did and when, what it cost, and what we are changing.

What we changed

No next-day reheating of cooked meat. Temperature logs checked weekly. Food handler illness reporting with paid sick leave.

If nothing in the system changes, the report was only paperwork.

When do you declare it over? A common rule is when no new cases appear for twice the maximum incubation period. For Salmonella that is about six days. Then write the report: what happened, the evidence, the actions with dates, and the cost. Most importantly, what changes. Here, no more cooking meat one day and serving it the next, weekly checks of temperature logs, and a rule that food handlers report illness, with paid sick leave so they are not tempted to hide it. If nothing in the system changes, it will happen again.

Three things to carry out of this room

An outbreak is more cases than expected. You need a baseline to know.

Compare the ill with the well. The line list, the epi curve and the attack rate table do this.

Act on a reasonable suspicion. Control and communication start on day one.

Three things to take away. First, an outbreak is more cases than expected, so you need routine data to know what is expected. Second, the method is always the same: compare the ill with the well, using the line list, the epi curve and the attack rate table. Third, as a manager, do not wait for proof to protect people. Act on a reasonable suspicion, and communicate early. Questions?
